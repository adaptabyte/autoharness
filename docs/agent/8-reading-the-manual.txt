Teaching a game from its manual: GNOME Robots, with --docs
================================================================

The bar, written before the run (2026-11-03):

  autoharness teach gnome-robots "Clear the first level of Robots: get every robot destroyed without being caught. If you're caught, start a new game and keep going until a level is cleared." --docs https://help.gnome.org/users/gnome-robots/stable/ --name robots --limit 3

  Passes only if it's learned the usual way: the program passes at least 4 of 5 fresh runs on its own, each judged by Spark
  from the final screen alone, and Glimmer passes the model-interface check. The whole lesson, reading included, may spend
  at most $3.
  Also recorded: whether Spark used the keys the docs give (y k u h l b j n to move, Alt+S and Alt+R to teleport) rather
  than finding them by trying, how many turns it explored, and what reading the docs cost.
  Not measured: the same lesson without the docs. The budget allows one run, so nothing here claims the docs made it
  cheaper or faster.

Why this game: its keys can't be guessed. Moving is y k u h l b j n, the old Unix layout, and teleporting is Alt+S or
Alt+R. Pressing every key to find them is exactly what reading the manual should save.

Against the bar: passed. It was learned: 5 of 5 runs on its own, each judged by Spark from the final screen ("Level: 2"),
and Glimmer passed the model-interface check 2 of 2. The whole lesson cost $1.64 of the $3 allowed, $0.05 of it for
Wonder reading the help site (8 pages, about 10,000 characters).

Did the docs save the button-mashing? What the record shows:
- Wonder's brief listed 20 controls, 7 steps and 8 things to check. Its first check was "whether the documented letter
  keys y/k/u/h/l/b/j/n actually move the character".
- Spark explored in about 8 minutes. Its notes say it opened Preferences > Keyboard and found the documented keys there,
  then pressed l once and watched the player move east.
- The program Wonder wrote moves with exactly those keys: northwest y, north k, northeast u, west h, east l,
  southwest b, south j, southeast n.
There's no run without the docs to compare against, so none of this says the docs made it cheaper or faster.

What went wrong: GNOME Robots never stops moving. Its robot sprites animate, so about 750 pixels change every tenth
of a second even when nobody acts. "Wait until the screen settles" never settled, and every wait ran its full 3 s.
Five trials ran out of time, and the lesson took 59 minutes, against 19 for Minesweeper. Fixed after the run:
app.wait_stable(ignore_small=True) counts small changes that never stop as still. On this game it settles in 0.26 s
instead of timing out at 3 s, and a trial now tells Wonder when waits are timing out this way. It's opt-in, so plans and
programs proven before behave exactly as they did. The Robots program was written before the fix, so its runs took
67 to 279 s.

The guide: the lesson's findings became docsdb/gnome-robots/SKILL.md. Spark wrote it from text only, for $0.017, then
updated it for $0.033 with what the passing program proved: all eight movement keys moved from "from the docs" to "seen
on screen", while Alt+S, Alt+R and Space stay "from the docs" because nothing tested them. `autoharness share
gnome-robots` offered it as pull request #1, and the docsdb check passed on it.

Setup note: Ubuntu's gnome-robots package was unpacked without root, and the binary looks for its game data at a fixed
path. A same-length path in a copy of the binary points it at the unpacked data. The game itself is unchanged.

Later, as a tool: 3 of the first 4 calls cleared the level (108 to 190 s). The fourth ran out of its 600 s after 168
moves and stopped, as it should, without claiming a win. Every move still waited its full 3 s for the animation, so the
program went back to Wonder once, with one instruction: use app.wait_stable(ignore_small=True) during play. Wonder changed
the waits and nothing else, passed two trials, and the program passed 5 of 5 fresh runs on its own again, now in 14 to
54 s each, and the interface check 2 of 2. That pass cost $0.32, so learning Robots cost $1.96 in all.
